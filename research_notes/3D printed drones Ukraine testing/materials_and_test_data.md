## Q1. How do printed airframes compare quantitatively with injection-moulded, foam (EPP/EPO) and carbon-fibre/composite airframes on weight, stiffness, impact tolerance and cost?

### Takeaway
Measured coupon data show that conventional (unreinforced or chopped-fibre) FDM and SLS plastics are limited to about 40-50 MPa tensile strength. Continuous-fibre composites (printed or laid up) reach 4x or more of that and about 40x the matrix stiffness. Printed PETG and similar frames are consistently reported as markedly less stiff, less vibration-resistant and lower in impact-energy absorption than carbon-fibre frames. Their advantages are cost, speed, logistics, and repairability, not structural efficiency. Direct quantitative comparisons with EPP foam and injection-moulded frames are almost absent.

### Cited Findings

**Coupon-level strength and stiffness (printed vs. continuous-fibre composite)**
- Šančić, Brčić, Kotarski, Łukaszewicz, *Materials* 2023, 16(14), 5060 [peer-reviewed]: for FDM and SLS specimens, tensile strength values reach up to about 40 MPa, whereas for continuous filament fabrication (CFF, Markforged-type, fibreglass reinforcement) it "can be at least four times higher", and the gap grows with fibre proportion. The paper's stated aim was to compare CFF materials with aluminium alloys for loaded multirotor airframe parts. — [PMC10384317](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10384317/); see also [TVZ record](https://www2.tvz.hr/publikacija/experimental-characterization-of-composite-printed-materials-for-the-production-of-multirotor-uav-airframe-parts/). Note: one search summary said tensile, three-point flexural and Charpy impact were all used. Another read the abstract as saying only tensile results are shown, with the other two tests "preliminarily investigated". Check the full text.
- Follow-up study, Hrčak 2026 [peer-reviewed, abstract only]: FDM-printed PLA and PETG and CFF-printed Onyx with fibreglass were tested against a commercial reference frame/arm. CFF specimens showed superior stiffness but were "considerably more expensive and required significantly longer production times". — [Hrčak record](https://hrcak.srce.hr/hr/343671)
- Markforged composites datasheet [manufacturer datasheet]: Onyx (nylon with chopped carbon) tensile modulus 1.4 GPa (ASTM D638), tensile yield about 36 MPa. Continuous carbon fibre reinforcement: tensile strength 800 MPa, tensile modulus 60 GPa (ASTM D3039). — [Markforged composites datasheet (reseller copy)](https://3dverkstan.se/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/markforged_composites_datasheet.pdf). A secondary source gives Onyx as 35 MPa / 2.4 GPa, which conflicts with the 1.4 GPa figure, so treat 2.4 GPa as unverified. The datasheet is dated (2018 upload) and manufacturer-measured.
- Powder-bed nylon reference values [manufacturer datasheets]: SLS PA12 about 50 MPa (XY) and 42 MPa (Z), elongation at break 11% (XY) — [HLH Rapid SLS PA12 spec sheet](https://hlhrapid.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/SLS-PA12-White-Spec-Sheet-HLH-Rapid.pdf). SLS/MJF PA12 about 48 MPa, modulus 1,650-1,800 MPa, elongation 15-20% — [Xometry PA12 SLS/MJF sheet](https://xometry.pro/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/Nylon-PA12-SLS-MJF.pdf).
- A "generative design for FDM-printed UAV frames" review, EPJ Web of Conferences 2026 (RIAC T2026) [conference review]: of 57+ studies reviewed, generative design achieves 15-50% mass reduction vs conventional designs, but FDM still faces anisotropic behaviour and fatigue limitations. — [EPJ abstract](https://epjwoc.epj.org/articles/epjconf/abs/2026/23/epjconf_riact2026_01003/epjconf_riact2026_01003.html)
- Review: "Composite Filament Materials for 3D-Printed Drone Parts" (PMC) [peer-reviewed review] describes carbon-fibre-infused PLA, PETG and nylon as giving improved strength-to-weight performance, "rivaling traditionally machined counterparts". This is a review-level claim without matched-geometry test data in the summary. — [PMC12155913](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12155913/)

**Frame-level comparisons (PETG / carbon-fibre-filled vs pure carbon fibre)**
- Al-Hadithi and Alcón Flores, *Drones* 2025, 9, 789 [peer-reviewed]: PETG-based designs, "even when reinforced with short carbon fibers", offer about a 23% increase in yield strength over neat PETG but "remain substantially inferior in specific stiffness and vibration resistance compared to pure carbon fiber frames". The authors propose a dual-track strategy: PETG for rapid low-cost prototyping, carbon-fibre composite as the benchmark for production-level performance. They combined static and transient nonlinear FEM (impact at 40 km/h) with bench and in-flight trials. The summary did not reveal drop heights, energies or flight numbers. — [MDPI Drones 9/11/789](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-446X/9/11/789)
- Same-group related work, MATEC Web of Conferences 2024 [conference, simulation]: FEA of ABS, PETG and nylon frames, including a simulated 1 m drop for a redesigned frame. Specific results not retrieved. — [MATEC 2024](https://www.matec-conferences.org/10.1051/matecconf/202440604016)
- ManaFly / MakerWorld frame pages [vendor/hobbyist, simulation]: a 10 N load simulation estimated a PETG frame about 61% less stiff than a carbon-fibre plate, and a PET-CF variant about 29% stiffer. The carbon-fibre arm was estimated to absorb about 70% more impact energy. The page itself admits that the models assume isotropic, purely elastic material, which printed parts are not. — [MakerWorld 2512262](https://makerworld.com/models/2512262), [MakerWorld 2000546](https://makerworld.com/models/2000546) (attribution approximate between the two)
- Kyiv-Mohyla Academy bachelor thesis, T. Hrynchuk 2025 [thesis, abstract only]: FPV drones with mostly 3D-printed frames were compared with conventional frames on flight performance, structural strength and noise. Finding: printed frames "in some cases" can be a reliable alternative to composite frames. Materials and numbers not retrieved. — [UKMA repository](https://ekmair.ukma.edu.ua/handle/123456789/36535)
- Reference composite frame benchmark: Khan, Deng, Khan, Fu, arXiv:2509.09024 (Sept 2025) [preprint]: a single-tow architected continuous-fibre frame weighed 260 g at 31.2% fibre volume fraction with a 30-minute manufacturing time and was about 10% lighter than a DJI F450 frame (authors credit about 3 min extra flight time). A drop from 100 m (255.1 J) caused no visible damage, and a 150 m drop was reported to fail at a central arm joint. This is a continuous-fibre process, not an FDM thermoplastic airframe, so it is a composite benchmark rather than direct printed-plastic evidence. — [arXiv 2509.09024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.09024)

**Weight vs foam**
- McMillan field test (2021, reported by Fabbaloo) [press, informal test]: the same 60-inch RC wing was printed in standard PLA (about 600 g, noticeably heavy in flight) and in colorFabb LW-PLA (foaming PLA). The LW-PLA wing was 40 g lighter than the traditional foam wing (332 g) and handled almost identically. Single wing, no EPP or crash testing. — [Fabbaloo](https://www.fabbaloo.com/news/3d-print-materials-count-a-field-test-of-lightweight-pla)
- eSUN ePLA-LW [vendor/marketing]: density "up to 0.54 g/cm3" and claims of lower weight than KT board, EPP and PP board builds. Not independently measured. — [eSUN](https://esun3d.com/news/esun-new-lightweight-pla-launched-for-rc-planes-and-cosplay)

**Cost and production**
- Formlabs [vendor/marketing]: printed drone frames at about $59 each, vs $239 outsourced and $115 on a legacy SLS process; printing avoids six-figure tooling for injection moulding. — [Formlabs drones page](https://formlabs.com/uk/industries/drones/)
- TriMech [vendor/marketing]: an SLS PA12 drone airframe on a Formlabs Fuse 1+ took 11 h 32 min of print time and cost about $33.16 per drone in build cost. No flight-durability data. — [TriMech](https://mfg.trimech.com/from-powder-to-payload-how-trimech-3d-printed-a-functional-drone-for-tradeshow-flight/)
- Unionfab guide [vendor guide]: injection moulding only becomes cheaper per unit at high volumes. — [Unionfab](https://www.unionfab.com/blog/2025/07/3d-printed-uav)
- Ukraine War Analytics [secondary/unverified]: FPV frame printing is described as the most significant scaled use of 3D printing in Ukraine, and printed PETG is stated to have about 30-50% of the tensile strength of injection-moulded equivalents. This is the only printed-vs-injection-moulded strength figure surfaced, it is not sourced to a test, and the page's other figures (about 1 million FPVs in 2024; $300-500k annual filament cost at 10% printed share) are also unsupported. — [Ukraine War Analytics](https://ukraine-war-analytics.com/technology/3d-printing-spare-parts-field.html)
- US Army DEVCOM ARL "SPARTA" printed drone [press, programme statement]: total cost "just over $1,000"; the replaceable printed airframe absorbs most crash damage while electronics are reused. — [VoxelMatters](https://www.voxelmatters.com/us-army-research-lab-develops-3d-printed-drone-co-designed-with-soldiers/)
- TAF Drones [press]: more than 100 staff reported to produce about 1,000 drones a day using manufacturing technologies that include 3D printers. — [3D Printing Industry](https://3dprintingindustry.com/news/ukraine-deploys-3d-printed-drones-to-combat-russian-shahed-swarms-242362/)
- Share of printed airframes: Meticulous Research claims 50-70% of Ukrainian drones use 3D-printed airframes [secondary/unverified, methodology unknown]. — [Meticulous Research](https://www.meticulousresearch.com/meticulous-next/meticulous-next-3d-printed-attritable-drones-outlook-2026-2034). Not corroborated elsewhere.
- Alternative attritable airframes: a Wood Central story says Russian volunteer plywood-framed drones can cut manufacturing cost by 10-15%, and one report judged a Ukrainian plywood drone more scalable than 3D printing or fibreglass [press/secondary]. — [Wood Central](https://woodcentral.com.au/russia-uses-chinese-plywood-for-kamikaze-drones-in-ukraine/); cardboard (SYPAQ) context: [Defense News](https://www.defensenews.com/unmanned/2023/09/13/see-the-ultralight-cardboard-drone-donated-to-ukraine/)

### Inferences
- Reading the Materials 2023 data with the Markforged datasheet: unreinforced/chopped-fibre printed thermoplastics (about 35-50 MPa, 1.4-1.8 GPa) sit one to two orders of magnitude below continuous-fibre laminates (800 MPa, 60 GPa) in stiffness. So any printed airframe is a stiffness-limited, geometry-compensated design (thick sections, ribs, shells) rather than a like-for-like CF replacement.
- The mass advantage of printing comes from design freedom (15-50% claimed in the generative-design review) and from foaming filaments for wings (about foam-weight in one informal test), not from the base material's specific strength.
- The competitive case in the Ukraine context rests on unit cost, tooling-free iteration, and repair or replace-in-the-field, not on measured structural superiority. No source quantifies crash survivability per dollar against EPP or injection-moulded frames.
- EPP's known crash resilience is not quantified by anything I found, so any claim that printed airframes match or beat EPP in impact tolerance is unsupported by public data.

### Gaps
- No controlled head-to-head (same geometry, same load case) test of printed vs injection-moulded vs EPP vs CF airframes found.
- No numeric drop/impact energy for PETG, PA-CF or ASA FDM frames retrieved (Drones 2025/UPM paper numbers not visible; full text should be read at [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-446X/9/11/789) and the UPM copy at [oa.upm.es/92277](https://oa.upm.es/92277/)).
- No injection-moulded drone-frame cost per unit; the Formlabs figures are vendor claims.
- Wichita State NIAR has published Markforged material-qualification reports (CAM-RP-2023-008; NMS 754-1 slash sheet), which are the closest thing found to an aerospace-style qualification dataset for printed composites. Existence only; numbers not read. — [NIAR CAM-RP-2023-008](https://wichita.edu/industry_and_defense/NIAR/Documents/CAM-RP-2023-008-RevNC-Markforged-OFRA-CFRA-MPDR-11.7.2023.pdf), [NMS 754-1](https://wichita.edu/industry_and_defense/NIAR/Documents/NMS-754-1-Rev-B_Slash-Sheet_11-13-23.pdf)

## Q2. What failure modes are documented (layer delamination, creep/warping in heat, brittleness in cold, vibration cracking, water absorption), and what mitigations are used?

### Takeaway
The best-documented failure mechanism is anisotropy/inter-layer weakness in FDM (tensile strength across layers is often about half of in-plane or lower), followed by heat softening of PLA (glass transition about 60 C) and moisture and UV ageing of nylon and PETG. Quantified evidence for cold embrittlement, vibration fatigue, and chopped-carbon-nylon fatigue life is largely missing from what could be retrieved.

### Cited Findings

**Anisotropy and layer delamination (print orientation)**
- ABS, ASTM D638 [peer-reviewed, older study]: tensile strength 23.81 MPa at 0 deg vs 11.21 MPa at 90 deg, about a 53% reduction (my arithmetic). — [Mechanical Anisotropy Behavior of FDM ABS](https://repository.unimal.ac.id/59)
- FacFox knowledge base [vendor/marketing]: FDM parts lose "more than 55%" of strength in the Z direction (no study cited). — [FacFox](https://facfox.com/docs/kb/how-3d-printing-direction-orientation-affects-strength)
- Shapeways support article [service-bureau guidance]: tensile strength can differ by up to 4-5x between Z and XY loading. — [Shapeways](https://support.shapeways.com/hc/en-us/articles/17839359742876-How-does-part-orientation-affect-the-strength-of-3D-printed-parts). This is much larger than the other figures and may refer to worst-case materials.
- Azcaray et al., *Applied Sciences* (DOI 10.3390/app16199401; the summary labelled it 2025, though the DOI suggests the 2026 volume) [peer-reviewed]: FFF PETG tensile response is by far the most orientation-sensitive property; compressive response is almost insensitive to orientation; PETG keeps a larger share of its fracture resistance (mode I) in the weak transverse orientation than ABS does. — [CiteDrive record](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/anisotropy-and-defect-tolerance-of-fff-manufactured-petg-a-combined-tensile-compressive-and-mode-i-fracture-study/)
- Powder-bed contrast [manufacturer datasheet]: SLS PA12 about 50 MPa XY vs 42 MPa Z, about 16% lower (my arithmetic). — [HLH Rapid](https://hlhrapid.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/SLS-PA12-White-Spec-Sheet-HLH-Rapid.pdf)
- No anisotropy data for ASA or PA-CF were returned. The UAV generative-design review also names anisotropy as a main FDM limitation. — [EPJ abstract](https://epjwoc.epj.org/articles/epjconf/abs/2026/23/epjconf_riact2026_01003/epjconf_riact2026_01003.html)

**Heat: creep, softening, warping**
- PLA glass transition is about 60 C (65 C by DMTA for a printed PLA); the modulus drops sharply through the transition region, with one article reporting that in the steepest part (50-75 C) a 5 C rise can cut modulus by 210-310 MPa. — [PMC9738534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9738534) (attribution approximate; see also [VSB study of PLA temperature resistance](https://dspace.vsb.cz/items/9e018af2-d31c-4597-9955-1e7add49796d))
- PLA heat-deflection temperature is roughly 50-55 C in a vendor table; creep grows with temperature; standard PLA is "increasingly risky above 50-60 C". — [JLC3DP](https://jlc3dp.com/blog/pla-temperature-resistance) [vendor]
- Annealing helps PLA only at low loads; under high enough load annealed specimens deform at the same temperature as unannealed ones. — surfaced in the same search; attribution approximate ([PMC9738534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9738534))
- QIDI blog [vendor/marketing]: PETG Tg about 75-80 C, ASA about 100-105 C, ASA creeps less above about 30 C ambient; ASA needs a heated chamber (about 40-70 C) to print without warping. — [QIDI](https://qidi3d.com/blogs/print-lab/asa-vs-petg-filament-direct-sunlight)
- Drop-weight impact of FDM PETG (layer-height study) was tested at ambient temperatures of 20, 40 and 60 C (results not retrieved). — [PMC9230522](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9230522) (attribution approximate)

**UV**
- QIDI [vendor, explicitly "estimated", no method]: ASA retains about 85-90% of properties after UV exposure vs about 70-80% for PETG, which "tends to yellow and embrittle". Treat as marketing-grade. — [QIDI](https://qidi3d.com/blogs/print-lab/asa-vs-petg-filament-direct-sunlight)
- 2024 study of additively manufactured PA12 (Ghimouz et al.) [peer-reviewed/thesis; attribution approximate]: temperature, irradiance, relative humidity and exposure time significantly affect ultimate tensile strength and Young's modulus. No retention figures retrieved. — [ETS Montreal repository](https://espace2.etsmtl.ca/29437/1/Kenne-JP-2024-29437.pdf)

**Moisture / water absorption**
- SLS PA12 datasheet [manufacturer]: water absorption about 0.5% at 20 C / 50% RH and about 2.0% after 24 h boiling (ASTM D570). — [HLH Rapid](https://hlhrapid.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/SLS-PA12-White-Spec-Sheet-HLH-Rapid.pdf)
- Polymaker [manufacturer guidance]: PA12 reaches moisture equilibrium at a lower percentage than PA6; moisture conditioning changes dimensions and properties. — [Polymaker nylon](https://ca.polymaker.com/pages/nylon)
- NIST study of laser-sintered PA12 [government lab]: reports the effect of environmental humidity on Tg, plus tensile and failure-mechanism findings. — [NIST](https://www.nist.gov/node/1578206)
- A conference abstract compares PA12-CF and PA6-CF at 55% RH for about two months (moisture uptake, then flexural, tensile and impact). Results not found. — [ProgramMaster abstract](https://programmaster.org/PM/PM.nsf/ApprovedAbstracts/DD563F878DE42D2085258AD60059DF5B)

**Fatigue and vibration**
- No S-N data for chopped carbon-nylon FDM retrieved. Closest: University of Messina estimated the fatigue limit of FDM PA12 + 15% carbon fibre using infrared thermography and energy methods (agreement between methods reported; no numbers in the snippet) — [iris.unime.it](https://iris.unime.it/handle/11570/3341278); Northumbria studied fatigue of continuous-fibre carbon-nylon (different process) — [Northumbria](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/fatigue-analysis-of-additive-manufactured-long-fibre-reinforced-n/); a 2023 *Polymers* paper tested PAHT CF15 vs unreinforced PA statically (tension, compression, bending), no fatigue — [PMC9823968](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9823968/).
- Vibration: the only direct statement is that PETG frames, even carbon-filled, are substantially inferior to pure carbon frames in vibration resistance — [MDPI Drones 9/11/789](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-446X/9/11/789). The Kyiv-Mohyla thesis compared noise across frame designs (no numbers retrieved) — [UKMA](https://ekmair.ukma.edu.ua/handle/123456789/36535).

**Brittleness and toughness (anecdotal)**
- A vendor blog says standard PLA is too brittle for structural parts and recommends PA-CF for frame arms. — [Additive-X](https://www.additive-x.com/blog/3d-printing-for-drones-materials-parts-and-applications) [vendor]
- UAV-modelling blog [anecdote]: nylon beats PETG in elongation and impact, and nylon frames often survive crashes that bend PETG (no data). — [UAVModel blog](https://blog.uavmodel.com/?p=5899)

**Field repair and replaceability (programme statements)**
- Army DEVCOM ARL SPARTA: printed airframe is replaceable and absorbs crash damage; electronics are reusable after the next airframe print. — [VoxelMatters](https://www.voxelmatters.com/us-army-research-lab-develops-3d-printed-drone-co-designed-with-soldiers/)
- April 2025 soldier-produced 3D-printed FPV drone with experimental EW capability evaluated at Hohenfels, Germany; design includes salvageable parts that transfer to a new drone after impact. — [Yahoo/Army report](https://www.yahoo.com/news/army-wants-3d-printed-drones-175527657.html). An Army task-force member said the team had to learn how to get the right print quality and ensure the airframe is the right weight and strong enough to fly. — [Manufacturing.net](https://www.manufacturing.net/supply-chain/news/13105419/army-to-test-3dprinted-drones-during-combat-exercises)
- Ukraine: printers reportedly let units fix a design or technical flaw at the front quickly (manufacturer claim, 2023) — [The Defense Post](https://www.thedefensepost.com/2023/11/23/ukraine-3d-printed-drones/). Battalion-level maintenance cells printing and repairing frames [secondary/unverified] — [Ukraine War Analytics](https://ukraine-war-analytics.com/technology/3d-printing-spare-parts-field.html). Danish volunteers supporting Ukrainian drone-factory printing: [ing.dk](https://ing.dk/artikel/3d-printing-ukrainian-drone-factories-get-help-danish-volunteers).

**Mitigations named in sources**
- Fibre reinforcement (CF-filled PETG about +23% yield; PAHT-CF15; continuous CFF) — see Q1 sources.
- Material choice by environment: ASA over PETG for sun and heat, PA-CF over PLA for arms, PETG or composite filaments in high-stress zones (vendor/hobbyist sources above).
- Generative or topology-optimised design for mass (15-50% claimed), shell designs with internal reinforcement — [Design Society paper](https://designsociety.org/download-publication/39723/Implementation+of+lightweight+design+in+the+product+development+process+of+unmanned+aerial+vehicles).
- A peer-reviewed SLS UAV (Journal of Aviation, 2020) was designed, built in SLS and flown in three weather conditions (abstract only) — [Acikerisim FSM record](https://acikerisim.fsm.edu.tr/items/028cc95c-b2cd-4f10-97d4-1c0564e6f2cc/full).
- Drying and annealing/conditioning for nylon (Polymaker guidance above).

### Inferences
- PLA is clearly unsuitable for airframes exposed to sun, vehicles or summer ambient; its softening range (about 50-75 C) overlaps credible payload-bay and sun-soak temperatures. This is from the sources' Tg/HDT figures plus general reasoning; no source measured airframe temperatures.
- Powder-bed PA12 (SLS/MJF) is much less orientation-sensitive (about 16% Z penalty) than FDM (50% or more), so for crash-loaded parts powder-bed nylon is likely the lower-risk printed option. FDM parts need layer direction aligned with principal loads.
- Nylon's moisture sensitivity is a more important variable for PA6-based filaments than PA12, per the Polymaker statement. Quantified retention figures are missing.
- Cold performance is entirely unquantified in what I found. For a winter war theatre this is the largest single evidence gap among the failure modes.
- Vibration cracking is plausible given the lower stiffness and damping reported for PETG frames and the fatigue limitations flagged in the review, but no test data quantify it.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data on low-temperature (sub-zero) impact or tensile behaviour of FDM PLA/PETG/nylon/PA-CF; one search specifically for -20 C returned nothing.
- No S-N or vibration-fatigue data for printed airframe materials; no motor-vibration measurements on printed arms.
- No numbers on PA6/PA12-CF moisture-conditioned property retention, and no quantified UV weathering data (QUV/xenon) beyond the vendor "estimates".
- No independent field-failure statistics (e.g., breakage rates) for printed frames in Ukraine; all field-repair claims come from vendors, press, or programme officials.
- No resin (SLA/DLP) data on UAV airframes retrieved (an area that was in scope but not covered), nor TPU data, nor MJF-versus-SLS comparative flight-test data.

## Q3. What does published research say about radar, IR and acoustic signature of plastic airframes versus metal or carbon ones, and what are the limits of those claims?

### Takeaway
The only solid measured evidence is Semkin et al. (IEEE Access 2020): at 26-40 GHz, larger carbon-fibre multirotors were easier to detect than plastic-built ones, and the LiPo battery was a dominant reflector. No peer-reviewed RCS comparison of a 3D-printed UAV against a metal airframe was found, and nothing at all was found on IR or acoustic signatures of printed airframes.

### Cited Findings
- Semkin et al., "Analyzing Radar Cross Section Signatures of Diverse Drone Models at mmWave Frequencies", *IEEE Access* 8, 48958-48969 (2020) [peer-reviewed]: quasi-monostatic RCS of nine multirotors plus a single Li-ion polymer battery, 26-40 GHz in 1 GHz steps. Construction material affects RCS; larger carbon-fibre drones are easier to detect and plastic drones are harder; the battery is a major reflector. — [UCLouvain record](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/handle/2078.5/111840), [Aalto record](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/analyzing-radar-cross-section-signatures-of-diverse-drone-models-/), [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10379104). Open dataset: [IEEE DataPort](https://ieee-dataport.org/comment/3027) (DOI 10.21227/ry87-xf51; platforms include DJI Phantom 4 Pro, F450, Mavic Pro, Matrice M100, a custom hexacopter and a bare HMF600 tricopter frame).
- A secondary summary of that work quotes about +7 dB higher mean RCS and +10 to +20 dB higher peak RCS for fibre-heavy drones vs mostly-plastic ones. [secondary/unverified; check the paper] — the search summary did not say which page carried this figure; candidates are the [AB Corp article](https://abcorp.com/3d-insights/why-carbon-fiber-drones-lose-wars/) or the [NSF PAR copy of the paper](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10379104) (attribution approximate; verify against the paper, DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2020.2979339).
- *Drones* 2025, 9(7), 499 [peer-reviewed; summary only]: adding metallic microparticles to printed plastic (PLA, ABS, PETG) raises volumetric conductivity at low density, to increase a drone's radar signature without changing its design. This implicitly shows that plain printed plastic is a weak radar reflector, but no measured RCS values were seen. — [MDPI Drones 9/7/499](https://www.mdpi.com/2504-446X/9/7/499)
- AB Corp (Additive Business) article "Why Carbon Fiber Drones Lose Wars" [vendor/marketing]: promotes HP MJF PA12 as a lower-radar-visibility alternative to carbon fibre. — [AB Corp](https://abcorp.com/3d-insights/why-carbon-fiber-drones-lose-wars/)
- Ukraine press coverage of printed drones (Defense Post, 3D Printing Industry, Fabbaloo, VoxelMatters) discusses cost, speed and iteration; the summaries surfaced no signature or detectability data. — e.g. [The Defense Post](https://www.thedefensepost.com/2023/11/23/ukraine-3d-printed-drones/)

### Inferences
- The Semkin result is for mmWave (26-40 GHz) and for multirotors, not for fixed-wing or the longer-wavelength search radars used for air defence. It cannot be read as a general statement that printed drones are "stealthy" or that plastic reduces detection against X-band or lower-frequency radars.
- Because the battery, motors, wiring, flight controller and any metal fasteners dominate returns in small UAVs (Semkin finding on the battery), airframe material alone probably changes RCS modestly. This is my reasoning from the battery finding, not a measured result for printed airframes.
- Marketing assertions (AB Corp) that printed PA12 is a low-signature option rest on the carbon-vs-plastic contrast and are not backed by a published RCS campaign of printed UAVs.
- Acoustic and IR signatures are determined mostly by motors, props and batteries; this is general reasoning, no source retrieved.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed RCS measurement of a 3D-printed UAV vs an equivalent metal or carbon-fibre airframe; no X-band, S-band or VHF data.
- No IR/thermal or acoustic signature study for printed airframes; the Kyiv-Mohyla thesis compares noise but numbers were not seen.
- The +7 dB/+10-20 dB figures are from a secondary summary only.

## Q4. What testing frameworks exist in the Ukraine war context (Brave1 testing, Ukrainian MoD codification/certification, NATO-aligned standards), and what did they find, if public?

### Takeaway
Ukraine's public framework is: Brave1 / MoD class-specific field-testing methodologies, followed by NATO-style codification (NSN assignment), which is required for state procurement, and then ordering via Brave1 Market. The public readouts concern mission performance (range, guidance, comms, EW). I found no published material-level or airframe-structural findings, and nothing specific to 3D-printed airframes.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- From what is public, Brave1/MoD testing is mission-effectiveness testing in near-combat conditions rather than materials qualification; airframe material is only indirectly assessed through survival in service. I could not read the methodology pages, so this is an inference from press readouts.
- Codification (NSN assignment) is a supply-system gate, not evidence of a structural test regime; I found nothing linking it to STANAG 4671-type airworthiness criteria or to mechanical test specifications for printed parts.
- Because procurement on Brave1 Market is lowest-price among compliant bids, there is a plausible incentive for low-cost printed airframes, but no source tests that link.
